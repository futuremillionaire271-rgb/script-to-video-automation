Number three, warm milk with a pinch of nutmeg. Your grandmother wasn't wrong about this one, there's real biology behind why warm milk has been a bedtime tradition for generations. Milk contains tryptophan, an amino acid that your brain uses as the starting material to build serotonin, which your body then converts into melatonin as darkness falls. Milk also delivers calcium, and calcium plays a role in helping your brain use that tryptophan efficiently. The warmth itself matters too, a warm drink slightly raises your skin temperature and then, as that heat dissipates, helps trigger the natural drop in core body temperature that your body associates with the start of sleep. To make it, gently warm about a cup of milk, dairy or a fortified plant milk both work, until it's warm but not scalding, and stir in a small pinch of ground nutmeg, which has a very mild sedative reputation in traditional use, plus a little natural sweetness so you don't need sugar. Sip it slowly around sixty minutes before bed rather than right as you're getting under the covers. If you're lactose intolerant, a lactose-free milk or unsweetened oat milk gives you a similar tryptophan and calcium profile without the digestive discomfort. It's simple, it's inexpensive, and for a lot of people, the ritual of a warm cup itself becomes part of what signals the body that it's time to slow down. That's not just a nice feeling, it's actual sleep science, doing the same calming routine at roughly the same time every night trains your brain to associate that sequence of actions with the approach of sleep, the same way a bedtime story once worked for you as a child. Many people assume only cow's milk can deliver this effect, but the tryptophan and calcium combination matters more than the specific source, so a calcium-fortified oat or almond milk gives you a very similar benefit if dairy doesn't agree with you.

Number two, a warm magnesium mocktail. Out of everything on this list, this is the one with the most specific research behind it for people exactly your age. Magnesium is involved in regulating your nervous system, and it works partly as a natural calming agent on receptors in your brain that get overactive when you're stressed, tired, or in mild pain, all things that commonly keep older adults from settling into sleep. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, forty-six elderly adults with diagnosed insomnia were given either five hundred milligrams of magnesium or a placebo daily for eight weeks. By the end of the trial, the magnesium group showed a meaningful jump in sleep efficiency, rising from about seventy-five percent to eighty-five percent, their total sleep time increased by roughly seventeen minutes on average, the time it took them to fall asleep dropped by about seven minutes, and they experienced fewer early morning awakenings compared with the placebo group. Part of why this works so well specifically for older adults is that magnesium intake and absorption both tend to decline with age, meaning a lot of people over sixty are running a mild, unrecognized deficiency without ever being tested for it, so topping it up isn't just a sleep trick, it's correcting something that may already be running low. To build this into a warm drink rather than a pill, dissolve a magnesium supplement formulated for sleep, magnesium glycinate is a good option since glycine itself has its own calming reputation, into a small cup of warm water, following the dosage on the label, and stir in a splash of tart cherry juice for flavor and a bit of natural sweetness. Drink it about an hour before bed. If you have kidney disease or take medications that affect your magnesium levels, check with your doctor before adding a daily magnesium supplement, since your kidneys are responsible for clearing excess magnesium and that process can work differently as you age. If you'd rather get magnesium through food than a supplement, pumpkin seeds, almonds, and spinach are all naturally rich sources, and a small handful of pumpkin seeds alongside your evening routine can be a reasonable food-based alternative, even if it won't match the precise dosing used in the clinical trial. As for which form of magnesium to choose, glycinate and citrate are generally considered gentler on digestion than magnesium oxide, which some people find causes stomach upset, so it's worth reading the label rather than grabbing whatever bottle is cheapest.

Number one, tart cherry juice, specifically the Montmorency variety. This is genuinely one of the most studied natural sleep aids for people your age, and unlike many trendy remedies, the research keeps holding up. Tart cherries are one of the very few natural food sources that actually contain melatonin itself, not just the building blocks for it. In a pilot study of older adults with chronic insomnia, participants who drank a tart cherry juice beverage showed significant improvements across their sleep measures compared to when they drank a placebo, with the most notable change being less time spent awake after initially falling asleep. A separate study measured melatonin levels directly, healthy adults who drank about an ounce of concentrated tart cherry juice thirty minutes before their evening meal for seven days showed significantly elevated melatonin along with increased total sleep time. And in a smaller trial focused specifically on adults fifty and older with chronic insomnia, drinking about a cup of tart cherry juice in the morning and another cup one to two hours before bed for two weeks was associated with an increase in sleep time of well over an hour on average. Beyond sleep, tart cherries are rich in anthocyanins, the deep red pigments that give them their color, which have been studied for their role in lowering markers of inflammation in the body, and plenty of people who drink it regularly report their joints and muscles feel less stiff in the morning as a side benefit. To use it, look for one hundred percent tart cherry juice, not a cherry-flavored cocktail loaded with added sugar, check the label for words like Montmorency or tart cherry concentrate rather than just sweet cherry, since the beneficial compounds are more concentrated in the tart variety than the sweeter kind most people eat fresh. Drink about four ounces roughly an hour before bed, and if straight juice feels too strong or too sweet for your taste, diluting it half and half with plain water still delivers a meaningful dose while going easier on your blood sugar. If you're diabetic or watching your blood sugar closely, tart cherry juice does contain natural sugars, so talk with your doctor about how it fits into your daily carbohydrate count, and consider diluting it with water to soften the effect. This is the drink I'd point almost anyone over sixty toward first, because the research base behind it, specifically in people your age, is the strongest of anything on this list. It's also worth mentioning how this compares to popping a melatonin pill from the pharmacy shelf, some people find that high-dose synthetic melatonin supplements leave them feeling groggy the next morning, since many over-the-counter capsules contain far more melatonin than your body naturally produces in an entire night. Getting a smaller, more natural amount from whole tart cherries alongside the fruit's other beneficial compounds tends to feel gentler for a lot of people. As with anything on this list, give it a real trial, most of the research showing meaningful results used it consistently for one to two weeks before the effect became clear, so don't judge it after a single night.

Now let's talk about how to actually drink these so they work with your body instead of against it, because timing and volume matter just as much as which drink you choose. First, you don't need much. Four to eight ounces is plenty for any of these, whether it's a small glass of tart cherry juice, a warm mug of chamomile tea, or a modest smoothie. Drinking more won't deepen your sleep any further, it will just mean an earlier trip to the bathroom. Second, timing matters, aim to finish your drink somewhere between forty-five and ninety minutes before you actually get into bed. This gives your body time to begin digesting it and lets any melatonin or magnesium start working before you're lying down, rather than still processing a full glass while you're trying to fall asleep. Third, thicker, richer drinks like warm milk or a kiwi smoothie tend to stay in your system longer and are less likely to send you straight to the bathroom compared to thin, watery drinks, so if nighttime urination is your main problem, lean toward those first. Fourth, try to use the bathroom on a consistent schedule each night, right before you get into bed, regardless of whether you feel the urge. Your bladder responds well to routine, and a consistent pre-bed bathroom visit can reduce how often you wake later in the night. Fifth, sip slowly rather than drinking it all in one go, taking about ten minutes to finish your drink gives your body a gentler, more gradual signal to wind down rather than a sudden rush of fluid. And finally, introduce just one new drink at a time, try it for a full week before switching to the next one on this list, since everyone's body responds a little differently, and giving each one a fair trial is the only way to know which one is truly working for you. A couple more things worth building in alongside whichever drink you choose. Try to prepare and drink it in the same spot, at roughly the same time, every single night, since that consistency itself becomes part of the sleep signal your brain learns to recognize, the same principle behind the warm milk ritual we talked about earlier applies to every drink on this list. And while you're sipping it, keep your phone, tablet, or television off if you can, the bright blue-toned light from screens can suppress your body's own melatonin production, which works directly against whatever your drink is trying to help with, so a dim lamp and a quiet room while you sip does more for you than people usually realize. Think of these few minutes as a small, deliberate wind-down ritual rather than just another task to check off before bed.

Sleep after sixty doesn't have to mean staring at the ceiling at two in the morning wondering why you're awake again. Your hormones have shifted, your bladder signals differently than it used to, and your nervous system reacts to stress and tension a bit more easily than it did decades ago, but none of that means restful, uninterrupted sleep is out of reach. The five drinks we covered today, chamomile tea, a kiwi bedtime smoothie, warm milk with a pinch of nutmeg, a magnesium mocktail, and tart cherry juice, aren't just comforting rituals, each one delivers a specific, researched nutrient your body can actually use to ease into deep sleep and stay there. Whether it's magnesium calming an overactive nervous system, melatonin from tart cherries working directly with your body's sleep clock, or tryptophan slowly converting into the hormones that guide you into rest, the right drink works with your biology instead of fighting against it.

The real takeaway here isn't to drink more before bed, it's to drink smarter. Pick one drink from this list that sounds appealing to you, take it at the right time, roughly an hour before you turn in, and give your body a full week to respond before judging whether it's working. Some people notice a difference the very first night. For others, it takes a few nights of consistency before their body catches on to the new routine.

Conditions like sleep apnea or an enlarged prostate can look a lot like ordinary restless sleep on the surface, but they need a different kind of evaluation and treatment altogether, one that a bedtime drink alone can't provide.
